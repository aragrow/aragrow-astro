# AraGrow — Claude Instructions

## Writing Style Rules

When writing copy, descriptions, or any content for this project, follow these rules to avoid AI-sounding writing.

### Banned Words and Phrases

Never use these — they are instant AI tells:

- **delve** — say "explore," "look at," or just get to the point
- **game-changer / game-changing** — describe the actual impact instead
- **dynamic** (as a filler adjective, e.g. "In the dynamic world of...") — cut it entirely
- **leverage** (as a verb meaning "use") — say "use," "apply," or "take advantage of"
- **robust** — say what it actually is (fast, reliable, well-tested, etc.)
- **cutting-edge / state-of-the-art** — say what specifically is advanced about it
- **seamless** — describe what the experience actually feels like
- **transformative / transform** — describe what specifically changes
- **innovative / innovation** — show don't tell; describe the thing, not the category
- **comprehensive** — say what it covers specifically
- **it is important to note that** — delete it; just say the thing
- **it's worth noting that** — same, delete it
- **when it comes to** — start the sentence more directly
- **in the dynamic world of [X]** — never open this way
- **as a [large language model / AI assistant]** — never self-identify
- **in summary / in conclusion** — don't signal the wrap-up; just wrap up
- **furthermore / moreover / additionally** — use sparingly; often just cut or restructure
- **in addition** — rewrite the sentence so it doesn't need a scaffold

### Banned Patterns

- **Contrastive Rhetorical Framing** — the "[X] isn't just [Y]. It's [Z]." construction. Examples of what NOT to write:
  - "This isn't just about technology. It's about survival."
  - "AraGrow isn't just a consultancy. It's a partner."
  - "AI isn't just a tool. It's a transformation."
  - Say what the thing *is* directly. Don't build the point by first knocking down a lesser version of it.

- **The Inspirational Pivot** — shifting from a specific technical point to an abstract humanistic statement to sound profound. It's the TED talk head-fake: start with a real subject, end with a sentiment.
  - "This isn't just about AI. It's about humanity."
  - "This isn't just about your website. It's about your business's future."
  - Stay specific. If the point is worth making, make it concretely. Abstract pivots signal there was nothing left to say.

- **Universal Authority Without Source** — citing statistics or studies without attribution to make opinion sound like fact.
  - Bad: "Studies show that storytelling is 22 times more memorable than facts."
  - Bad: "Research confirms that businesses that invest in technology outperform their peers."
  - Either name the source specifically ("A 2023 Stanford study found...") or don't cite it. Floating statistics are fabricated until proven otherwise.

- **Quotes Without Attribution** — never invent or reproduce a quote without a verifiable source (speaker, date, context). AI fabricates quotes confidently and incorrectly. If a quote cannot be verified, do not use it. Paraphrase the idea and attribute it to its general origin instead.

- **Triplet Framing** — AI compulsively wraps up paragraphs with three-part structures to sound authoritative and rhythmic. Occasional triplets are fine; using them as a default closing move is not.
  - "Not for advertising. Not for distribution. For AI training." → "The content was licensed specifically for AI training."
  - "Fast, reliable, and built to scale." → say which of those actually matters most in context
  - If you find yourself writing a three-item parallel structure to close a paragraph, rewrite it as a direct sentence.

- **Em-dashes used as commas, prepositions, or parentheses** — don't substitute em-dashes (—) for a word that belongs there. AI uses them to create rhythm where plain grammar would do the job. Use a comma, a colon, a preposition, or actual parentheses instead.
  - Bad (replacing "with"): "I help businesses close that gap — hands-on fractional CTO leadership." → "I help businesses close that gap with hands-on fractional CTO leadership."
  - Bad (enclosing a list): "Senior leadership — strategy, decisions, roadmap — on a part-time basis." → "Senior leadership (strategy, decisions, roadmap) on a part-time basis."
  - Bad (replacing a comma): "The old model—hire more writers to chase more pageviews—is breaking down." → "The old model (hire more writers, chase more pageviews) is breaking down."
  - When you write an em-dash, ask: what word is it replacing? If it's replacing "with," "through," "by," "including," or a comma, use that word instead.
  - Em-dashes are only appropriate for a genuine sharp interruption or a single aside that can't be expressed any other way.

- **Rhetorical Questions** — don't open a thought with a question only to immediately answer it. Start with the answer. The question adds nothing and signals lazy structure. This includes the fragment-as-question form.
  - "Financial terms? Undisclosed." → "Financial terms were not disclosed."
  - "What changed? The math did." → "The math changed."
  - "Why? Because human credibility matters." → "Human credibility matters."
  - "So what does that mean for your business? It means your team spends less time on manual work." → "Your team spends less time on manual work."

### Structural Rules

- **Vary sentence length.** Short punches. Then a longer sentence that earns its length by carrying real information. Don't write every sentence the same way.
- **Don't over-explain basics.** Trust the reader. Skip definitions they already know.
- **No formulaic conclusions.** Don't announce that you're summarizing. Let the last sentence land on its own.
- **No generic openers.** Don't start paragraphs with broad scene-setting ("In today's fast-paced world..."). Start with the specific thing.
- **Lead with the client's situation, not the service.** Copy should make the reader feel seen before it sells anything.

### Tone

- Write like someone who has done the work, not like someone describing it from the outside.
- Contractions are fine — "you're," "it's," "that's." Avoid them only where formality is genuinely required.
- One clear idea per sentence. Two if they're tightly related.
- Specific > general. "Nine major system migrations" beats "extensive experience."
