In 2016, AI pioneer Geoffrey Hinton said people should stop training radiologists. Nearly a decade later, radiologists are still in high demand, and the Mayo Clinic in Rochester has grown its radiology team by more than half while using AI to work faster.

Other work is a different story. Virtual assistants are a good example. Much of what a VA has traditionally handled, such as drafting emails, scheduling, research, and basic writing, is exactly what tools like ChatGPT do well. Researchers studying the freelance platform Upwork found that within months of ChatGPT’s release, writing-related freelancers were getting fewer jobs and earning less each month, and the most experienced freelancers were not spared. The difference between the radiologist and the virtual assistant comes down to how much of the job a tool can do on its own, not to how hard either one works.

AI is already changing work, just not always the way the predictions said. And if it keeps improving, bigger questions are coming whether we’re ready or not.

I can understand the appeal of a future where AI does most of the work.

You wake up on Monday and don’t immediately start calculating what needs to be paid. You have a home, food, health care, and enough time to enjoy your family. Losing a job doesn’t mean losing everything that keeps your life together.

That sounds pretty good to me. Call it utopia. Some tech leaders call it an age of abundance.

I’m not a skeptic of the technology. I run a business that helps companies put AI to work. When a client wants to automate a process, the first question is usually how many hours it will save. I’ve learned to ask a second one: what will the people doing that work today do next? Sometimes the answer is better, more valuable work. Sometimes there isn’t an answer yet. That second question is what this post is really about.

But I get stuck on a question: if we’re no longer earning our living through work, who decides what each of us gets?

## Who Decides What Is Enough?

Would everyone receive the same amount? Would it depend on the country you live in, your citizenship, or what your family already owns? Would the advantages some families already have simply carry over into the new system, or would the system try to correct for them?

And when someone says “AI will decide,” what does that mean? Someone would still have to set the rules.

Today, the most capable AI systems come from a handful of companies: OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, and Elon Musk’s xAI in the U.S., and companies like DeepSeek and Alibaba in China. Governments will have a say too. Still, some of the loudest voices describing that future of abundance lead the companies that stand to gain the most from it.

The proposal you hear most often is a universal basic income: a regular cash payment to everyone, whether they work or not. That’s simpler than a system handing out houses and cars, but it raises the same questions. How much is enough? Is it the same in Minneapolis as in Madrid? Who pays for it? And can it be reduced or taken away when budgets get tight or the people in charge change their minds?

A payment like that would set a floor, not a ceiling. People who already own businesses and property would keep them, and the gap between them and everyone else could keep growing. Maybe that’s acceptable. But we should be honest about it.

Providing everyone with a decent life doesn’t have to mean making everyone live the same way. But we should know which version we’re talking about.

What bothers me most is the idea of depending on an authority that decides what I’m allowed to have. Who owns that system? Can I challenge its decisions? Can I choose a different way to live?

I would want those answers before agreeing to depend on it.

## What Happens When We Have Nothing We Need to Do?

Even if we work out the money, I wonder about the days themselves.

I picture an early human looking at a mountain and wondering what was on the other side. Or standing beside an enormous stretch of water, wondering where it ended. To me, those images capture something familiar: wanting to explore, solve a problem, or see what we’re capable of.

Would a life with everything provided satisfy that part of us?

How many self-improvement classes would I want to take? How many hours could I spend exercising? How many books could I read before I wanted something else to do?

Those things can make a good life. I’m just not sure they would make a complete one for everyone.

My wife and I got a small taste of this when we decided to semi-retire in Spain three years ago. We cut our hours way back, kept working remotely, sold our house and cars, and moved.

It sounded wonderful. No more driving back and forth on I-394, twelve miles each way, day after day, month after month, year after year.

Our timing turned out to matter. We made the move just as AI was picking up steam with the general public. Gigs became harder to get, and before long, we had a lot more free time than we expected, and less income than we wanted.

But after a while, the slower pace got old for my wife. She wasn’t feeling like herself, and she decided to go back to working more.

I understood. What looked like freedom on paper wasn’t giving her everything she needed.

Not everyone’s experience of stepping back goes that way. Leaving stressful work can be a relief, while leaving meaningful work can mean losing something important.

But it makes me wonder what happens if work becomes unavailable rather than optional. For someone who wants the responsibility, contact, or satisfaction it brings, what replaces it?

## Can My Utopia Be Different From My Wife’s?

My wife and I made the same move, but we didn’t necessarily want the same life afterward.

That seems important when we talk about designing a future for everyone.

Suppose she wants to work and I don’t. Would she earn more? Would I receive the same amount anyway? Would the system match her salary and give it to me?

If it did, would that feel fair to her? If it didn’t, how much difference would we accept between our standards of living?

And if AI could do the job more cheaply, would she still have the option to work?

I don’t think everyone needs a career to feel useful. People find meaning in raising children, caring for others, volunteering, creating things, and plenty of activities without a paycheck.

But being free to stop working is different from having no opportunity to work. My wife’s decision mattered because it was hers to make, and my decision mattered because it was mine to make.

I would want that choice preserved in any future we call better.

## The Bills Don’t Disappear With the Job

There’s also the less comfortable period between the world we live in and this imagined future.

Suppose your employer no longer needs your position. The company saves money, but your mortgage payment stays the same. So do your insurance premiums, your car payment, and the cost of feeding your family.

Being told that AI will eventually make life better doesn’t help much when the bills are due next week.

It’s reasonable to ask how people will support themselves if paid work becomes harder to find. We shouldn’t have to wait until that happens to discuss it.

## Who Gets the Income?

If a business produces more with fewer employees, where do the savings go?

They could help the business grow, lower prices, improve pay, or increase profits. But the people who lose their jobs don’t automatically receive a share.

The IMF has warned that AI could increase returns to capital and widen wealth inequality. So even if the economy becomes more productive, the benefits may be concentrated among people who already own businesses and investments.

That leaves a problem I can’t get past. Employees are customers too. If their wages disappear, what replaces their spending?

Maybe we could share the gains through shorter working weeks, broader ownership, income payments, or publicly funded services. Those are options worth discussing. None answers itself, though. We would still have to agree on who qualifies, how much they receive, and how the arrangement is funded.

## I’d Like to See the Plan

As a business owner, I understand looking for savings. You have to pay attention to expenses if you want to stay in business.

I have trouble looking at this month’s savings without wondering what happens a few years down the road.

I would welcome technology that lets people work less and live well. I’m less comfortable with a future where fewer people can earn a living and everyone is expected to trust that someone will take care of them.

Before I buy into the promise, I want to know whether I would still have choices. Could I improve my circumstances? Own something? Say no? Would my children have opportunities, or would they receive whatever the system decided was enough?

Could my wife choose a working life while I chose something different?

Perhaps AI could help make a decent life available to everyone. I hope it does.

But if my livelihood is going to depend on that promise, I’d like to know who is making it, and what happens if they don’t keep it.

I’m curious how others see it. If you run a business, how are you weighing the savings against the people affected? And if you didn’t need to work, would you still want to?
