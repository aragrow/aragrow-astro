Most online stores have a contact list somewhere. A spreadsheet, a note on someone's phone, a few numbers saved in a group chat. What most stores don't have is a list they have actually tested.

A communication plan answers one question before you need it: when something breaks, who do we call, and will they pick up? This post covers what to put in that plan, a simple layout that works for teams of any size, and the step most stores skip, which is calling every number on the list a few days before the Black Friday and Cyber Monday weekend.

## Why it matters on Black Friday and Cyber Monday

When checkout stops working on a normal Tuesday, a slow response costs you a few orders. From Black Friday through Cyber Monday, every minute spent looking for a phone number is a minute of your highest sales of the year going somewhere else. And remember that the biggest platform outage of last season didn't land on Black Friday at all. It hit on Cyber Monday.

The problems are rarely dramatic. They look like this:

- The developer who knows the server is traveling for Thanksgiving, and nobody has their personal number.
- The hosting support line saved in the spreadsheet is two years old.
- Three people open tickets with the payment provider at once, and nobody knows who is in charge.
- The person who can approve an emergency change is unreachable, so the fix waits.

Each of these is a communication problem, not a technical one. And each one is cheap to prevent if you find it before the week starts.

## What goes in the plan

A communication plan is a contact roster organized by area of responsibility. For each area, you name one person who owns it, one backup, and the outside vendor you would call. Every name gets a phone number that has been confirmed to work.

This is the same layout used in business continuity and incident response plans: one row per area, owner first, backup second, vendor third. It works because during an incident, people don't search by name. They search by problem: "payments are down, who do I call?"

### The areas to cover

Most stores need these areas. A small store may have one person covering several of them, and that's fine, as long as each area has a backup.

- **Decision maker:** approves emergency changes and spending
- **Communications owner:** writes and sends customer updates
- **Website and store platform:** the storefront, checkout, and extensions
- **Hosting and servers:** where the site runs
- **Domain, DNS, and SSL:** what keeps the site reachable and trusted
- **Payments:** gateway and processor
- **Shipping and fulfillment:** warehouse, carriers, and label printing
- **Customer service:** email, chat, and phone support
- **Marketing and email:** campaigns, email platform, and ads
- **Security:** suspicious activity, fraud, and account lockouts

A few rules make the roster work:

- **Personal phone numbers, not just work email.** The whole stretch from Thanksgiving to Cyber Monday is a holiday weekend.
- **Write down where account numbers live, not the numbers themselves.** Point to your password manager instead.
- **Print it.** If your email or shared drive is down, a paper copy still works.
- **Leave the last column blank for now.** You'll fill it in during the call test.

## Four days out: make the calls

A roster is only a plan once you've tested it. On Monday, November 23, four days before Black Friday and a week before Cyber Monday, call every number on the list. Not an email, not a group message. A real call.

Why four days? It's close enough that the numbers reflect who will actually be working the holiday weekend, and far enough out that you still have time to fix what you find. One test covers the whole run, from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday on November 30.

Set aside an hour and work through the roster in order:

1. **Call each owner.** Confirm they answer, that they know which area they own, and that they'll be reachable Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday.
2. **Call each backup.** Same questions. Ask when they'd step in if the owner doesn't answer.
3. **Call each vendor support line.** Confirm the number still works, how long the hold is, and what they'll ask you for, such as an account number or PIN.
4. **Test after-hours numbers after hours.** If a vendor's emergency line only works at night or on weekends, call it then.
5. **Record the result in the last column:** the date, who you reached, and anything that needs fixing.

Keep each call short. You aren't running a meeting, you're checking that the line works and the person knows their role.

### Introduce your callers before they have to call

If an area involves an outside company, such as your hosting provider, your agency, or your payment processor, don't let the first contact happen during an outage. Two steps, done before the holiday week:

- **Tell the vendor who will be calling.** Send a short note: these two people, the owner and the backup, are authorized to contact you about our account during the holiday weekend. Many vendors will not discuss an account with a name they don't have on file, so add both as authorized contacts on the account now.
- **Tell your team who talks to the vendor.** The roster already names who owns each vendor relationship. Share it, so three people don't open three tickets for the same problem.

The goal is simple: nobody at the vendor should be surprised by Bob calling when Bob has never talked to them before. Create that link early. A known name on the account gets a faster and smoother response than a stranger with an urgent voice.

## Why one phone number is never enough

People are reliable. Phones are not. Every one of these has happened on teams I've worked with, including my own phone:

- **Do Not Disturb was on.** During an emergency, my own phone was set to Do Not Disturb, and the call never rang.
- **The battery died.** A coworker's phone died the evening before, and he didn't notice until morning.
- **The phone was left at the office.** A coworker went home for the night and left the phone on his desk.
- **The phone was left in the car.** Another coworker's phone spent the night in the car on his night off.

None of these people were careless. They were living normal lives on a normal evening. The plan has to expect that.

Here are five more situations that are just as common, especially over a holiday weekend:

- **The number changed.** Someone got a new phone or switched carriers, and the roster still has the old number.
- **They're on a plane or out of signal.** Thanksgiving travel means flights, long drives, and family homes with poor coverage.
- **The call was marked as spam.** Many phones now silence or block unknown numbers, so a call from a coworker's personal phone may never ring.
- **They don't work there anymore.** The person on the roster left the company or changed roles, and nobody updated the list.
- **The vendor line moved.** The support number now goes to a chat-only queue or has a two-hour hold during peak week.

### How the plan covers each of these

| What goes wrong | What the plan does about it |
| --- | --- |
| Do Not Disturb or silenced unknown callers | During the call test, everyone saves the whole roster as contacts and adds them to their Do Not Disturb exceptions. Most phones also have a setting that lets a second call within a few minutes ring through. Turn it on. |
| Dead battery | Everyone on the roster charges their phone every night of BFCM week and keeps a charger or battery pack nearby. The backup covers it if the owner still can't be reached. |
| Phone left at the office or in the car | The backup is called next. No single phone is the only way to reach an area. |
| Number changed | The November 23 call test catches it, while there is still time to update the roster. |
| On a plane or out of signal | Each person tells you during the call test when they'll be traveling, so you know to go straight to the backup during those hours. |
| Person no longer there | The call test finds them, and the area gets a new owner the same day. |
| Vendor line moved or long holds | The call test confirms the number and the hold time. You find the priority support option or a named account contact before you need it. |

### Add one rule: when to move to the backup

The roster only works if everyone knows when to stop waiting. Agree on a simple rule and write it at the top of the roster:

1. Call the owner. If there's no answer, call again right away.
2. Send a text that says it's urgent and which area is affected.
3. If there's no response within 10 minutes, call the backup.
4. If neither answers within 20 minutes, call the decision maker.

That way, a dead battery or a forgotten phone costs you 10 minutes, not the whole evening.

## After the calls: fix what you found

Almost every store finds something during the call test. A number that goes to voicemail, a backup who will be out of town, a vendor line that changed. That's the point of doing it early.

For each problem, fix it the same day:

- **A number doesn't work:** get the right one and call again to confirm.
- **An owner or backup won't be available:** name a new one and call them.
- **A vendor line changed or has long holds:** find the priority or emergency support option, or the name of an account contact.
- **Nobody owns an area:** assign it before the end of the day.

Then print the updated roster and share it with everyone on it, so the person answering at 2 a.m. on any day from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday has the same list as everyone else.

### Your communication plan checklist

- [ ] Every area has an owner and a backup
- [ ] Every person has a personal phone number on the roster
- [ ] Every vendor has a working support number
- [ ] Every number was called on November 23, and the result was recorded
- [ ] Every gap found during the calls was fixed and retested
- [ ] The final roster is printed and shared with everyone on it

## Pick one default channel

When something breaks, the first minutes are often wasted deciding where to talk. One person replies to an email thread, two more are in a text chain, and the developer is answering in a chat nobody else checks. Facts get retold from one channel to another, and every retelling loses something.

Decide the channel now, before the weekend. The phone call is the alert. The conversation happens in one agreed place.

### What worked for us on election night

For an election night, our team set up one ongoing online meeting that stayed open the whole time. The link was shared in advance, and anyone could jump in at any moment to raise or discuss an issue. Nobody had to dig through their inbox for an invite while something was broken.

The flow was simple:

1. An issue occurs.
2. Whoever sees it calls the appropriate people from the roster.
3. Everyone jumps on the standing meeting.

No second-hand information. Everyone heard the issue, and the solution, straight from the source. Nobody worked from a summary of a summary.

### Do the same for Black Friday and Cyber Monday

- Create one meeting link that stays open from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday. Most meeting tools let you reuse a personal room or a recurring link.
- Print the link on the roster, right next to the escalation rule, and pin it in your team chat.
- During the November 23 call test, have every person join the meeting once from their phone, so the first time is not during an outage.
- Keep the rule simple: the call gets you there, the meeting is where the problem gets worked.

## Tell-tale signs your plan has holes

Some holes you can spot just by reading the roster. Others only show up when you make the calls. Look for both.

### On paper: signs you can spot today

| Sign | What it really means | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The same name appears in four or more rows | One person is a single point of failure for half the store | Spread the areas out, or give each one a different backup |
| A row has no backup | That area has nobody when the owner is unreachable | Name a backup before anything else |
| The owner and backup are both off for Thanksgiving | The backup only exists on paper that weekend | Ask about holiday plans and pick a backup who is working |
| Only work emails or desk phones are listed | Nobody can be reached after hours | Add personal cell numbers |
| A vendor row has a website instead of a phone number | You'll be filling out a contact form during an outage | Find the support or emergency number and an account contact |
| No "last tested" date anywhere | Nobody knows if the numbers still work | Run the call test and record the date |
| The roster lives in one place, such as one laptop or one shared drive | If that place is down, so is the plan | Print it and give everyone a copy |
| Nobody can say who approves an emergency change | Fixes will wait for permission | Name the decision maker and a backup |
| Time zones aren't listed | Someone may get a call at 3 a.m., or you'll wait for someone who is asleep | Add a time zone column for remote team members and vendors |

### During the calls: signs that only show up when you dial

| Sign | What it really means | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The call goes to a full voicemail box | Urgent messages won't get through | Ask them to clear it, and rely on calls and texts |
| The person is surprised to be on the list | They don't know what's expected of them | Explain their area, and when they'd be called |
| The backup says "I don't have access to that" | The backup can answer the phone but can't fix anything | Give the backup logins now, through your password manager |
| The vendor asks for a PIN or account number nobody has | Support can't help you during an outage | Find it and record where it's stored |
| The hold time is over 30 minutes | Standard support won't be fast enough on Black Friday or Cyber Monday | Ask about priority support, or get a named account contact |

The most dangerous hole is the backup who can't actually do anything. A backup with a working phone but no access is a messenger, not a backup. Check access during the call test, not on Black Friday morning or Cyber Monday night.

## The plan is the calls

You can't control whether something breaks on Black Friday or Cyber Monday. You can control how fast the right person hears about it. A tested roster turns a stressful hour into a five-minute phone call.
