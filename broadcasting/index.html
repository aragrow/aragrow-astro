Where Broadcasting Technology Actually Breaks Down

No tested backup plan or disaster recovery

If your CMS, servers, or core systems went down tonight, nobody can tell you how long it would actually take to get back up, or what you'd lose in the process.

What I do: I build and actually test the backup and disaster recovery plan, not a policy document nobody has verified works, so a bad night doesn't turn into a bad month.

Loss of tribal knowledge when someone leaves

The person who actually understands how your systems fit together might retire, take another job, or just be unreachable on the one night it matters, and none of it is written down anywhere.

What I do: I document what actually exists, not a generic IT binder, so the business isn't one resignation away from nobody understanding its own systems.

Reliability pressure with zero room for downtime

Breaking news, election night, and severe weather create traffic spikes your site has to survive with no warning.

What I do: I stress-test your infrastructure against the traffic patterns that actually break it, not average-day load.

Wire and data feed integrations

Your AP, NOAA, or election-data feeds are held together by aging or fragile code that nobody fully understands anymore.

What I do: I review those integrations for single points of failure and rebuild the fragile ones before they break on a night that matters.

M&A-driven system consolidation

An acquired station drags along its own legacy CMS, ad stack, or newsroom tooling, and someone has to decide what stays.

What I do: I make that call, sequenced so the newly acquired station never goes dark. I did this under real deadline pressure during a company-wide migration in 2020.

Vendor lock-in on broadcast-specific systems

Your traffic, automation, or newsroom platform contract feels expensive and rigid, but nobody internally has the standing to push back or evaluate alternatives.

What I do: I evaluate what your contract actually locks you into versus what's negotiable, and give you a real read on switching cost and risk instead of the vendor's own sales pitch.

No senior technical leadership dedicated to modernization

IT gets handled by whoever's available. Nobody owns the multi-year technology plan.

What I do: I become that senior technical decision-maker on a part-time basis, working directly with your GM or ownership. No account managers, no handoffs.

Budget structured for OPEX, not CAPEX

A full-time CTO doesn't pencil out at your size, but limping along on patched legacy systems has its own hidden cost.

What I do: I give ownership a real cost picture: what the current setup quietly costs in outages and lost ad revenue, against what a fractional CTO costs versus a full-time hire.

Fragmented content across your stations

One CMS runs across multiple properties, but every station has drifted from the others, so a fix in one place doesn't fix it everywhere.

What I do: I audit the CMS as it actually runs today and set one technical standard your stations build on, with room for local control, not a fork per station. I did this exact work across seven Hubbard Broadcasting stations.

Newsroom-to-web handoff friction

Reporters and producers still copy stories into the website by hand because the newsroom system and the CMS were never properly connected.

What I do: I build or fix that integration so stories publish automatically instead of through manual copy-paste under deadline.

Questions Station Leadership Actually Asks

How much do you already know about the specific newsroom software we run, versus learning it on our time?

I've spent 23 years building and maintaining the systems around a newsroom, including the CMS integrations that connect to whatever system sits on the other side. You get someone who recognizes a broken newsroom-to-web handoff and fixes it, without needing months to learn your specific vendor's tool first. Stories move from newsroom to site faster, with less manual work and fewer dropped updates.

Do you understand how traffic and billing systems connect to everything else in the operation?

I have direct experience evaluating what a traffic or billing vendor contract actually requires versus what's negotiable. You get a real read on your vendor relationship instead of the vendor's own sales pitch. Lower vendor spend, or the confidence that what you're paying is actually justified.

Have you dealt with playout or automation systems, or just the website and digital side?

My hands-on experience runs deep in CMS, data integrations, and infrastructure, with honest limits on playout and on-air automation. You get someone who tells you when a specialist engineer is the right call instead of overselling a broader scope. You spend money on the right expertise instead of paying twice to fix a wrong hire.

Do you understand FCC compliance, EAS integration, and the public inspection file?

I have working knowledge of how compliance requirements intersect with a station's technology systems, paired with honesty about not being a compliance attorney or EAS engineer. You get systems that reliably support compliance, plus a clear flag the moment something needs a specialist. Fewer compliance surprises, and the right expert brought in before a small gap becomes a real problem.

Have you worked across a multi-station group, or only single-property businesses?

I built and maintained a custom CMS running across seven TV stations at Hubbard Broadcasting. You get someone who has already lived the tension between one shared technical standard and each station's need for local control. A consistent, maintainable system across properties instead of a patchwork that drifts wider every year.

Do you understand how OTT and streaming fit into a station's digital strategy?

I treat OTT and streaming distribution as an infrastructure and integration problem, not just a content decision. You get a read on where your CMS and data feeds create single points of failure before they cause a Roku or Fire TV outage. Reliable content delivery across every screen your audience actually watches on.

Do you replace our current IT person, or work alongside them?

The engagement is built to take ownership of technology decisions and roadmap, working next to your existing IT staff. You get senior judgment added to your team, not a replacement for the people already doing the work. Are you the person who implements changes, or the person who tells someone else what to implement?

My model combines strategy with hands-on execution, not just recommendations. You get someone who writes the integration or rebuilds the fragile piece directly, not a report that sits in a drawer. Fixes actually happen, on a timeline you can see, instead of stalling after the strategy phase.

How available are you during breaking news, severe weather, or election night?

I stress-test infrastructure against your actual high-pressure traffic patterns ahead of time. You get systems built to hold up on the worst night of the year, with direct availability if something still goes wrong. Your site stays up during the exact moments your audience and advertisers are watching closest.

The engagement model is remote and async-friendly, built around splitting time between Minneapolis and Spain, with on-site available for key milestones. You get coverage across every market and time zone your station group operates in, plus in-person presence when it actually matters. Consistent technology leadership across your whole group, not just your flagship station.
